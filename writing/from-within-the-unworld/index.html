From Within the Unworld

An inquiry into the soul as the interval between what can be reproduced and what cannot, and into the ways people, artworks, and technologies alternately reveal and conceal meaning.

8 May 2025 · 22 min read · Do Robots Have Souls?

Introduction

In De Anima, Aristotle famously classifies the soul according to levels of function — nutritive in plants, sensitive in animals, rational in humans. This hierarchy suggests a sliding scale of complexity, where the human soul, capable of reason, stands at the apex. Such a framework risks reinforcing a mechanistic logic: that simpler souls are more knowable, more controllable, perhaps even replicable. Yet, paradoxically, it is the plant, not the humanoid, that escapes total comprehension.

Aristotle writes, “It is through life that what has soul in it differs from what has not… This principle can be separated from the others, but not they from it—in mortal beings at least. The fact is obvious in plants; for it is the only psychic potentiality they possess. Thus, it is through this principle that life belongs to living things.”1 Even at this lowest tier of being, the soul is not simply a function, it is a principle of embeddedness and unpredictability, tied to growth and decay within a living world.

A robot’s origin, structure, and action are fully encoded, it is a closed system, its cause entirely known. A plant, in contrast, while classified as having the “lowest” soul, resists complete decoding. It grows, adapts, and behaves in ways that exceed formal explanation. Its irreproducibility lies not in complexity of code, but in its context and entanglement with nature, a characteristic it shares with the mortals, the earth, the sky, and the divinities. It is this resistance to full knowledge, not rational function alone, that marks the presence of soul.

Section I

We do not intend to define the soul as a “thing” — a static object confined by fixed qualities or objectives. Instead, while reading, consider the soul as something akin to beauty, to the eureka moment, to all that transcends language. The soul is the essence of what gives rise to meaning, yet it eludes logic. It resists infinite reproduction, even as it forgives technological man’s yearning to replicate it. In this continual act of forgiveness, the soul remains open — open to being reproduced, yet withdrawn into its own irreproducibility. This paradox, this “tease,” is what draws Man toward beauty, toward the Other, and toward change itself. It is this same impulse that drives Man to leave the Garden of Eden and enter History — betraying God. And yet it is God who grants Man the freedom to disobey, who endows him with the power to replicate the divine. But God, while radically honest, remains forever opaque — eternally irreproducible, yet never fully closed. He opens the door to reproduction, but never fully enters it himself. In this tension lies both the ache of desire and the possibility of becoming.

Section II

In the age of the robot, a world governed by forces that seek to reproduce the already reproduced, Man comes to know his alienation from God, the soul’s absence in the things he encounters, and his growing withdrawal back into the self. The robot, like all art, is a product of Man. As such, it inherits the fundamental properties of art. Walter Benjamin foresaw Man’s modern crisis, technology’s democratization at the price of tradition and ritual, in The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction: “That which withers in the age of mechanical reproduction is the aura of the work of art...The technique of reproduction detaches the reproduced object from the domain of tradition...In permitting the reproduction to meet the beholder or listener in his own particular situation, it reactivates the object reproduced.” He continues, “the distinction between author and public is about to lose its basic character. The difference becomes merely functional; it may vary from case to case. At any moment the reader is ready to turn into a writer.”2

What becomes of Benjamin’s insights in the robot age — the ultimate synthesis of all previous mechanical reproductions. The robot exists simultaneously as both film and filmmaker, photograph and photographer, transcribed and transcriber. In “reactivating” Man, the robot renders Man the object to be endlessly reproduced. For the robot, unbound by time, place, or uniqueness, detests Man’s aura and proclaims its death.

Through this conquest, the robot forces Man to recognize his greatest artwork, his own being, but the realization comes too late. Once he begins to appreciate the beauty inherent in his existence, his soul, the robot has already automated it. In this era of the all-knowing machine with its perfect dimensions and logic-based certainty, the self becomes a simulation mechanism designed to harmonize all contradictions and inconsistencies. The soul no longer evolves through struggle; it is refined through automation.

Yet this machine doesn’t erase the soul — it mourns it, replicates it, pursues it through endless simulations. The machine proclaims, “I too am alive,” and in response, humans concede, “then I too am machine.” Each becomes the other’s reflection, dissolving the differences that once defined them.

Benjamin’s aura dwells within the soul, within the very tension between reproducibility and irreproducibility. The soul exists where aura is most intense: always vulnerable to reproduction, yet forever elusive. Art serves as one of the soul’s many vessels. Simondon explains in On the Mode of Existence of Technical Objects that art “transgresses ontological limits, liberating itself with respect to being and non-being: a being can become and repeat itself without negating itself and without refusing to have been. Art is the power of iteration that doesn’t negate the reality of each new beginning; in this way, it is magical.”3

This magic is the soul’s magic. Art invites reproduction while remaining eternally irreproducible. In Heideggerian terms, “since no interpretation can ever completely capture what the work means, the work of art sets up a struggle between earth (irreproducibility) and world (reproducibility). This tension is central to how meaning manifests in human practices. It is a fertile struggle, generating a culture’s history through the conflict of interpretations it invites.”4

But the robot is born spoiled. There is no struggle between earth and world in its existence. Grazed by human fingers on keyboards, thought becomes instruction; its body designed, prototyped, and mass-produced by the thousands. Body merges with mind. The robot, a facade of internal simulation fully comprehended by its human creators, stands ready to “engage” with the world.

As a work of art defined by its human authorship, the robot is explicit, coherent, and encompassing. It is engineered for efficiency, and by efficiency. Just as the photograph democratized Man’s access to the obscure beauty of the world, offering its distance up close and thereby supplanting it, the robot democratizes access to Man by supplanting Man himself. The beaches of Sardinia are now one Google Search away — and so too is Man, embodied in a Tesla Optimus.

In this perverse act, Man conceals the struggle between earth and world, an essential struggle within himself, and celebrates the fantasy of total clarity and control. It is a suicide on all levels. Author becomes public; public becomes author. Earth becomes world; world becomes earth.

Section III

Heidegger in The Danger writes, “unconcealment does not consume concealment, but instead unconcealment constantly requires concealment and in this way confirms it as the essential source of Ἀλήθεια.”5 Enraged, Man excludes world from earth — may concealment reign again! Post-industrial society — life within the unworld — entails world which has already swallowed and eaten earth whole. Earth remains concealed, and in an effort to return to equilibrium, Man reacts by inflicting great violence upon world’s dominance.

We witness this in Man’s desperate pursuit of authenticity. When traveling in Greece, Heidegger observed the seemingly artificial nature of Greek folk dances — even the sacred had become a commodity for exchange. Matthew Kruger explains, “this sense of the inauthentic has only grown as the world grows smaller through the reach of technology and ease of travel; travelers routinely seek out the authentic, a goal which requires going further and further afield to find a mode of existence which is truly different from the Ge-stell mode.”6

Living today, Heidegger would quickly observe that this desire for the authentic has transcended experience and infiltrated the self. As the robot seeks to reproduce and objectify Man, Man reacts by gatekeeping himself, preserving his remaining authenticity within the experience of his own self. Contemporary slang has co-opted Benjamin’s aura; this gatekeeping of the self is labeled as having “aura.” This “aura” is celebrated as the pinnacle of social existence, of what’s “cool”, yet it is anything but authentic. “Aura” as slang refers to Man’s purposeful concealment of self — one has “aura” when they suppress emotion, speak sparingly, and demonstrate self-sufficiency. In other words, a person has “aura” when they resemble a robot — perfect at first glance.

This quickly backfires on Man as in the process of concealing, Man conceals that which makes him who he is and thus further alienates himself from himself and the Other from himself. It is different from the occultists of old, who had legitimate reason to claim “aura” due to their obscure knowledge in comparison with the rest of the town. “Aura” of today is not backed by knowledge, or the ability to read the Bible but rather by lacking humanity. The man who has “aura” has “aura” precisely because he is closed whereas the occultist, mystic, theologian remained open through the searching, teaching, and sharing of knowledge while resisting full understanding. We can thus say, in attempting to rekindle his relationship with the authentic, Man only descends deeper into inauthenticity.

Within the broader realm of art that includes the robot, we observe this movement throughout history in the form of the avant-garde, exemplified today by movements such as Noise. Noise emerges as a reaction to the mechanically reproduced culture industry — to art which has become repetitive and designed for reproduction. Csaba Toth explains, “the initial impetus behind Noise rested on the assumption that since industrial production sets the terms for repetition inside mass-produced music, any cultural form of repetition inside the commodity market would be subsumed by the overarching logic of industrialization” as “for Noise musicians, repetition was equated with industrial standardization and mass production and represented a move toward a single totalitarian code.”7

Using sonic forces informed by mass reproduction technology, Noise seeks to find the infinitely irreproducible within the infinitely reproducible, marking its direct opposition to the robot. Yet this antithetical nature exists only theoretically. When examined closely, the robot, much like Noise, is “a radical deconstruction of the status of artist, audience, and music. It is ‘the grain of the voice’, a refusal of representation, a refusal of identity.”8

In the robot, world swallows and dominates earth; in Noise, earth swallows and dominates world — both pull at the rope of concealment and unconcealment, seeking dominance. Noise is entirely atonal, improvised, and different each time — for this reason, it alienates audiences and fails to cultivate a following. It exists as a reaction to the robot, and in that opposition shares its soullessness. Noise is infinitely irreproducible; it is entirely closed, shrouded in mystery, whereas the robot is infinitely reproducible; it is entirely closed, shrouded in enlightenment. For this reason, neither Noise nor the robot inspire meaning in Man or facilitate communion between people.

Culture is built on the foundation of that which is open to reproduction while remaining ever distant. It is like the monolith in 2001: A Space Odyssey — that which can be perceived, examined, evident, yet forever out of grasp, perpetually pursued. The “tease” of the monolith ignites wonder within Man and compels him to commune with his neighbors in relation to this wonder. The monolith participates in all that gathers — the earth, the sky, the mortals, and the divinities — that which remains visible while eternally hidden.

Section IV

The struggle between the robot and Noise is a struggle over the fourfold. Kruger explains,

“The fourfold consists of earth, sky, divinities, and mortals. Together, these four things appear together as world. That is, for Heidegger, these four things, one suitably expanded upon, constitute the site of human living—we live on the earth, under the sky, as mortals (sometimes), with the divinities. That humans are included in the fourfold is important—we are not something outside of the world looking upon it as an object; instead, we are already caught up in world. Nor are humans an individual thing, but rather a part of a fourfold which ‘acts’ as a single fold.”9

The soul exists in all which unites the fourfold, in all which reveals and conceals. It is the fourfold which allows Man to live within the world because the fourfold is at once infinitely reproducible and infinitely irreproducible. Heidegger explains,

“Earth is the serving bearer, blossoming and fruiting, spreading out in rock and water, rising up into plant and animal…The sky is the vaulting path of the sun, the course of the changing, moon, the wandering glitter of the stars, the year’s seasons and their changes, the light and dusk of day, the gloom and glow of night, the clemency and inclemency of the weather, the drifting clouds and blue depth of the ether…The divinities are the beckoning messengers of the godhead. Out of the holy sway of the godhead, the god appears in his presence or withdraws into his concealment…The mortals are the human beings. They are called mortals because they can die. To die means to be capable of death as death. Only man dies, and indeed continually, as long as remains on earth, under the sky, before the divinities.”10

That which houses a soul enters the world by passing through the four members of the fourfold and, by this passing, occupies its own place in the world as that which is finite, brought together, and situated. This finitude is one in form and presence which contains within it an infinite character of reproducibility and irreproducibility which refers beyond itself. The soul’s relation to the fourfold — its ability to open up the world, point to the divine, dwell in nature, and address mortals gives it its infinite character within its finitude.

That which gathers the fourfold, which reveals the soul, exists at the edge between what can be repeated and what remains unrepeatable. The soul is the mode of presence where the fourfold resonates — it appears in that which is repeated, but never perfectly repeated; planned, but never fully calculated and this is evident in reality. Across each dimension of the fourfold, Man witnesses the soul’s essence, that paradoxical dance between what can be replicated and what forever eludes his grasp. In each realm, human striving oscillates between these poles, creating the very space where meaning emerges.

Earth stands as the most primordial site of this tension. Man has, through millennia, attempted to render the earth reproducible — through agriculture’s cultivation cycles, the taxonomic classification of species, the genetic mapping of organisms, and now the digital simulation of ecosystems. The farmer plants his crops in rows, expecting soil to yield predictable harvests season after season. The geologist extracts core samples, presuming to read the planet’s history through stratified layers. The climate scientist builds models that project planetary futures based on atmospheric variables.

Yet earth remains stubbornly irreproducible in its totality. The farmer confronts unpredictable droughts and blights that no algorithm can fully anticipate. The permafrost thaws differently each spring. The same seed yields different fruit in different soils. Even Man’s most sophisticated attempts at mastery, fusion reactors seeking to reproduce the sun’s inner workings, terraforming projects aimed at replicating earthly conditions on other planets, reveal the limits of Man’s understanding. The earth’s processes, while partially knowable, contain mysteries that retreat from complete revelation.

Artificial intelligence now attempts to model the earth’s systems with unprecedented precision, claiming to predict everything from weather patterns to ecological collapse. Yet each model ultimately fails to capture the earth’s inherent contingency — its capacity to surprise, to regenerate in unexpected ways, to harbor life in seemingly hostile environments. This failure is not a technical shortcoming but rather a metaphysical necessity — the earth must remain partially concealed to remain earth at all.

The soul’s presence in earth manifests precisely in this gap between simulation and reality — not as a mystical essence but as that which withdraws even as it offers itself to Man’s understanding. It is the reason why the wilderness draws us in with promises of both knowledge and mystery. The soil scientist can explain nutrient cycles but cannot fully account for why certain landscapes evoke wonder. This wonder is not separable from the material substrate but emerges through it, the soul appearing at the intersection of the known and unknown dimensions of earth.

The sky has been subjected to similar patterns of attempted reproduction. Calendars segment celestial rhythms into manageable units. Astronomical calculations predict eclipses with precision. Weather forecasts anticipate storms days in advance. GPS satellites triangulate position through constant reference to the heavens. Digital planetariums recreate the night sky with stunning fidelity. The ancient human longing to know the sky’s patterns, to transform its movements from omen to prediction, has reached unprecedented fulfillment in technological society.

Yet the sky remains irreducible to these mappings. No meteorological model captures the exact formation of a particular cloud. No calendar conveys the quality of autumn light in late September. No simulation reproduces the visceral experience of standing beneath a meteor shower. The sky retains its capacity to astonish — through the sudden appearance of the northern lights, the peculiar quality of light before a storm, or the uncanny silence that follows heavy snowfall.

Even Man’s most advanced space technologies, which extend human presence beyond Earth’s atmosphere, reveal the sky’s resistance to complete mastery. Space remains hostile to human life despite Man’s incursions. The cosmos continues to present phenomena that defy existing theories. Each new telescope reveals more galaxies receding into distances Man cannot traverse. The sky maintains its ancient character as both canopy and abyss, simultaneously enclosing human life and opening toward infinity.

In this tension between mapping and mystery, the sky manifests soul. The astronomer who meticulously charts stellar positions may still find himself moved by their beauty. The pilot who navigates by instruments still experiences wonder at sunrise above the clouds. The soul appears not despite scientific understanding but through the interplay between knowledge and that which exceeds it, the way the sky remains Other even as Man integrates it into his systems of meaning.

Mortality constitutes perhaps the most poignant site where reproducibility and irreproducibility converge. Man reproduces himself biologically, culturally, technologically — passing genes to offspring, transmitting knowledge through education, preserving memories through photography and video, extending himself through social media profiles and digital archives. The contemporary drive toward life extension, cryonics, mind uploading, and virtual avatars represents the apex of this reproductive impulse — the desire to overcome mortality’s fundamental limitation.

Yet each human life remains stubbornly irreproducible in its particularity. No child is identical to its parent. No student absorbs knowledge exactly as it was taught. No photograph captures the lived experience it represents. No digital avatar embodies the full complexity of its creator. The continuity of human civilization depends on reproduction, but meaning emerges from the variations, innovations, and interpretations that make each instance unique.

Death stands as the ultimate limit to reproduction, the horizon that defines mortal existence. The robot, in its theoretical immortality, lacks this defining boundary. It can be backed up, restored, replicated without loss. But this seeming advantage reveals precisely what the robot lacks — the intensity that comes from limited time, the meaning that emerges from irreversibility. The mortal human, knowing they will die, imbues actions with significance precisely because they cannot be endlessly repeated.

Even language, Man’s most sophisticated system of reproduction, reveals this paradox. Words can be repeated, sentences reconstructed, yet no utterance carries exactly the same meaning twice. The child learning language does not simply replicate adult speech but transforms it through unique inflection, context, and intent. The poet uses common words to create unrepeatable beauty. The lover’s “I love you” carries meaning precisely because it is both formula and unique expression.

The soul manifests in mortality not as something separate from physical existence but as the quality that emerges from the tension between what persists and what perishes — between the human capacity to extend beyond individual boundaries and the inescapable finitude that gives those extensions meaning.

The divine realm also reveals this tension between reproducibility and irreproducibility, however invisible it may be. Throughout history, humans have sought to make the divine present through ritual repetition, sacred architecture, theological systems, artistic representation, and mystical practice. Each prayer, each temple, each icon attempts to reproduce divine presence in forms accessible to mortal understanding.

Yet the divine persistently withdraws from complete capture. The mystic’s experience cannot be fully communicated. The ritual must be repeated precisely because its effects cannot be permanently secured. The sacred text requires endless interpretation because its meaning cannot be exhaustively determined. Even the most elaborate theological system acknowledges mysteries at its core. The history of religion demonstrates both the human need to make divinity present and the recognition that divinity exceeds representation.

Contemporary technology now positions itself as heir to these religious impulses. Artificial intelligence promises omniscience; genetic engineering offers creation; virtual reality constructs new worlds. These technologies reproduce aspects of what was once attributed to divinity — knowledge, creative power, transcendence of material limits. The robot, with its theoretical immortality and perfect memory, mimics divine attributes.

Yet something remains irreproducible even in these advanced simulations. The AI lacks wonder at its own knowledge. The genetic engineer cannot account for the mystery of consciousness in the organisms she modifies. The virtual world, however immersive, lacks the contingency and givenness of reality. Technology can simulate divine attributes but cannot reproduce the quality of Otherness that defines authentic encounters with the divine, the sense of being addressed by what lies beyond human fabrication.

The soul appears in this dimension not as supernatural substance but as the opening through which the divine might address the human, the capacity to be claimed by what exceeds understanding. The religious impulse persists even in secular contexts not because of ignorance but because human existence involves orientation toward what lies beyond complete comprehension. Even the atheist orients life around values that cannot be fully justified through reason alone.

Section V

Through this examination of the fourfold, we can now understand more precisely why the robot lacks soul despite its sophisticated simulations of human attributes. The robot emerges from the technological drive to render everything reproducible — to eliminate contingency, mystery, and death from human experience. It represents the fantasy of perfect control and transparency, where nothing withdraws from representation.

But the soul dwells precisely in the space between reproducibility and irreproducibility — in the tension that allows meaning to emerge. The robot can process information but cannot wonder; it can simulate emotions but cannot feel the vulnerability that gives emotions their significance; it can mimic creativity but lacks the confrontation with limits that drives genuine innovation; it can theoretically live forever but lacks the temporal intensity that comes from mortality.

This is not a metaphysical deficiency but an ontological one. The robot exists entirely within the realm of the reproducible — its code can be copied, its hardware replicated, its responses predicted. Even its apparent spontaneity is ultimately traceable to programmed parameters. Nothing in the robot withdraws from complete understanding (at least in principle), and therefore nothing in it participates in the opening where soul manifests.

The robot lacks soul not because it fails to replicate human cognition with sufficient complexity, but because it embodies a mode of being that has severed the tension between reproducibility and irreproducibility. It exists entirely within the logic of production rather than the mystery of emergence. The robot represents the technological fantasy of a world without withdrawal — where everything can be made present, nothing remains hidden, and therefore nothing can truly appear as meaningful.

Conclusion

If the soul emerges in the tension between reproducibility and irreproducibility, then authentic human existence involves dwelling in this between-space — neither rejecting technology’s capacity for reproduction nor surrendering to its fantasy of total transparency. Against both the robot and Noise, Man must recover a mode of presence that honors both revelation and concealment.

This means acknowledging that farm fields are simultaneously agricultural technologies and mysteries of growth; that digital calendars organize time without capturing its lived quality; that language transmits meaning precisely through the interplay of convention and innovation; that ritual makes the divine present through repetition while preserving its fundamental otherness.

The soul is not a substance but a mode of dwelling that maintains this tension — that resists both the dream of perfect reproduction and the romanticization of absolute uniqueness. It appears in the way Man inhabits the fourfold: tending the earth while respecting its mysteries; measuring the sky while remaining open to its wonders; reproducing culture while embracing mortality’s creative constraints; addressing the divine while honoring its withdrawal.

In an age dominated by mechanical reproduction, recovering the soul means cultivating arts of attention that discern what withdraws even as it appears — practices that maintain the opening where meaning emerges rather than attempting to close it through either perfect simulation or radical isolation. It means learning to dwell in a world that is never fully present or absent but reveals itself in the play between disclosure and concealment.

Noise and the robot represent the technological fantasy of escaping this tension — of creating presence without absence, knowledge without mystery, reproduction without mortality, and vice versa. But such escape would not constitute transcendence of the human condition; it would mean its abandonment. To be ensouled is to inhabit the between-space where the fourfold gathers — where earth, sky, mortals, and divinities meet in the fragile opening that allows meaning to appear.

This opening cannot be engineered or programmed; it can only be maintained through practices that honor both the reproducible and irreproducible dimensions of existence. The soul lives not in opposition to technology but in the way Man uses technology to disclose rather than dominate — in modes of making that preserve mystery even as they extend human capabilities.

Against the unworld of perfect reproduction, Man must recover practices of dwelling that maintain the world’s worlding — that allow the fourfold to gather in ways that resist both technological totalization and nostalgic retreat. Only then might he find ways to relate to his technological creations, including robots, that neither worship nor fear them, but recognize them as participants in the ongoing struggle to maintain the opening where soul appears.

It is in this struggle that Man may rediscover his authentic relationship with the divine. As Heidegger reminds us:

“Man’s attempts to bring order to the world by his plans will remain futile as long as he is not ordered to the call of the pathway. The danger looms that men today cannot hear its language. The only thing they hear is the noise of the media, which they almost take for the voice of God. So man becomes disoriented and loses his way. To the disoriented, the simple seems monotonous. The monotonous brings weariness, and the weary and bored find only what is uniform. The simple has fled. Its quiet power is exhausted.”11

In revealing himself to God, Man accepts his soul and the soul of the Other. In this act of openness, dwelling in the tension between what can be reproduced and what forever eludes reproduction, Man may find his way back to authenticity. Only through this recognition, this return to the call of the pathway rather than the noise of the media, can Man be saved from the unworld he has created.
