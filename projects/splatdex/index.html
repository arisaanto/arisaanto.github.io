Splatdex

A Pokédex for splats

Personal project, 2026

The first Gaussian splats I encountered were Dany Bittel’s macro captures. I was drawn to the stillness of the subject, the movement available to the viewer, and the extraordinary detail that emerged up close. A fly could be approached from several directions, its hairs and wings available to an attention that would rarely be possible in passing. It recalled the fascination of natural history museums, where an animal usually glimpsed in motion can be observed at leisure.1

Collections give scattered encounters a place in a larger world. Albums, field guides, and cabinets of curiosities each offer a way of keeping things together, returning to them, and noticing relations between them. For me, that pleasure first took shape in sticker books, unlocking characters in LEGO Star Wars, and Pokémon’s Pokédex. Today, it finds another expression in photography.

Pokémon Go carried this pleasure into movement through physical places. I wondered what that connection between collecting and the world around us might become through splats. A shell found on a beach, a stone from a walk, or a keepsake at home could become a spatial image to keep and share. The collection could grow through new encounters as easily as through digitising objects already close to us.

Where a photograph fixes a view, a splat lets the viewer move around a reconstructed subject. A surface passes out of sight, another comes forward, and a detail begins to suggest a question. I wanted to give this kind of image a place to be kept, arranged, and read alongside other images. Splatdex explores this through catalogs, personal collections, and annotated views. I began the demo with downloadable captures from SuperSplat, then added objects scanned through the app.

An annotation gives a visible detail an explanation. A student reading about a strawberry’s achenes could locate them on the fruit, inspect their relation to its surface, and return to the whole. Notes keep the explanation close to what it describes.

I wanted curiosity to have somewhere to go from there. A conversation beside the note lets a reader ask why that structure exists or how it functions, with the object still in view. The AI assistant offers a way to follow those questions beyond the initial explanation.

In a classroom, a collection might accompany a chapter on plant anatomy or bring several examples into a shared discussion. For personal study, the same objects could sit alongside reading, with notes and questions gathered around them. Outside a course, an unfamiliar shell might be enough to begin. These are possibilities I wanted the interface to leave open, with looking, reading, and questioning supporting one another.

Research on interactive 3D displays suggests that both the views available and the way a learner encounters them matter. Active rotation helped object recognition in one VR study, while access to informative views explained benefits in another spatial task. These findings offer a starting point for exploring free rotation alongside annotated viewpoints.2 3

For my own collection, I used a phone to scan objects that had stayed with me from different journeys. These captures preserve their overall appearance, while the specialist macro captures reveal much finer detail. A reddish stone came from the Rota Vicentina in Portugal, walked with friends during my study abroad. The green-blue stone came from a stream near Bergün, toward the end of the Kesch Trek with a friend. The shell came from Wineglass Bay in Tasmania, where I spent last Christmas with my family. Their places in the collection carry something of those journeys.

A collection could hold personal histories as easily as information about an object. A shell might belong to a journal, a shared catalog, or a study guide. An annotation could record where it was found, explain its structure, or preserve a question to return to.

A useful precedent for this exchange is iNaturalist, where people record encounters with organisms through photographs or sound recordings, and other members help identify and discuss them. A personal observation becomes something others can learn from and contribute to. Its connection between field encounters and shared knowledge is especially relevant here. Splatdex asks what an explorable 3D image might add to that kind of exchange.4

Splatdex brings existing capture and rendering tools into an interface for collecting, arranging, and annotating spatial images. I imagine contributors extending the explanations around an object, with AI helping readers pursue their questions. In AR, these collections could occupy the space around us. Research into dynamic splats also points toward studying movement alongside form.5

The public demo includes editable annotations and an optional, experimental language model running in the browser. Capture is demonstrated with the iPhone prototype. Automatic recognition, generated notes, shared study spaces, and AR remain directions for the project.

Notes

  1. Dany Bittel, Macro Splats. The macro captures that introduced me to the medium. The original Gaussian Splatting paper describes the underlying representation and rendering method.
  2. James et al. (2002), “Active” and “passive” learning of three-dimensional object structure within an immersive virtual reality environment. Participants recognized objects faster after learning their structure through active rotation.
  3. Keehner et al. (2008), Spatial Reasoning With External Visualizations. In their cross-section task, access to relevant views accounted for benefits that control alone did not explain.
  4. About iNaturalist and How identifications work. Herodotou et al., Young people in iNaturalist, interviewed 31 young people about learning through participation online and in the field.
  5. Tzathas et al. (2026), Adaptive Spatio-Temporal 3D Gaussian Splatting for Scenes with Oscillatory Motion. Research on reconstructing changing scenes, including leaves moving in the wind.
