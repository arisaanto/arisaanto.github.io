Splatdex

A Pokédex for splats

Personal project, 2026

The first Gaussian splats I encountered were Dany Bittel’s macro captures. I was drawn to the stillness of the subject and the movement available to the viewer. A fly could be approached from several directions, its hairs and wings available to an attention that would rarely be possible in passing. There was something of the natural history museum in it, the chance to linger with a form usually glimpsed in motion.1

Sticker books and the Pokédex give collecting a form. Each entry belongs to a larger world, and each addition makes that world a little more familiar. Pokémon Go brought this impulse outdoors. I imagined a collection made from encounters with the world itself, where a shell, a flower, or a stone could become an entry to return to.

Where a photograph fixes a view, a splat allows movement through the views that were captured. A surface passes out of sight, another comes forward, and a detail begins to suggest a question. I wanted to give this kind of image a place to be kept, arranged, and read alongside other images. Splatdex takes the scans I found through SuperSplat and brings them into catalogs, personal collections, and annotated views.

An illustration in a book directs attention through its framing. Here, that framing can remain open to the reader. Notes offer points of entry, bringing a feature into view and connecting it to an explanation.

In one VR experiment, participants recognized objects faster after learning them through active rotation. In another spatial task, seeing informative views mattered more than controlling the display. These findings give the notes a purpose beyond labeling, as a way of bringing a useful view into reach.2 3

A question need not end at the annotation. Each note can open a conversation with an optional AI assistant, using the selected object and note as context. A reader can follow an unfamiliar term, ask why a feature matters, or pursue a connection beyond the initial explanation. The model runs in the browser; the annotations remain authored and editable.

For my own collection, I began with objects that had stayed with me from different journeys: a reddish stone from the Rota Vicentina in Portugal, walked with friends during my study abroad; a green-blue stone from a little stream near Bergün, toward the end of the Kesch Trek; and a shell from Tasmania. Their places in the collection carry something of those journeys.

The phone becomes a way into the collection. Moving around an object gathers overlapping views automatically, with pause and resume available while changing position.

After upload, the private Mac processor reconstructs the splat and returns it to the collection. The footage shows the capture of Tasmanian Shell I.

A collection could hold these personal histories as easily as it holds information about an object. A shell found on a walk might become part of a journal, a shared catalog, or a study guide. Reading about its structure, one could turn to the scan and trace what the text describes. Field observation and online exchange already meet in communities such as iNaturalist. I was interested in what a collection of spatial images might bring to that exchange.4

I imagine annotation becoming a shared practice, with contributors able to question, correct, and extend the explanations around an object. In AR, those collections could be arranged in the space around us. Research into dynamic splats also opens the possibility of returning motion to the image, so that an object might be studied through both its form and its movement.5

The prototype brings together catalogs, personal collections, anchored notes, and optional conversations with a browser-based language model. The iPhone prototype automatically captures overlapping, calibrated views. After upload, a private Mac processor estimates the camera positions and trains a Gaussian splat in Brush; the result returns to the collection. The current annotations are authored and editable. Automatic recognition, generated notes, shared study spaces, and AR remain directions for the project.

Notes

  1. Dany Bittel, Macro Splats. The macro captures that introduced me to the medium. The original Gaussian Splatting paper describes the underlying representation and rendering method.
  2. James et al. (2002), “Active” and “passive” learning of three-dimensional object structure within an immersive virtual reality environment. Participants recognized objects faster after learning their structure through active rotation.
  3. Keehner et al. (2008), Spatial Reasoning With External Visualizations. In their cross-section task, access to relevant views accounted for benefits that control alone did not explain.
  4. Herodotou et al., Young people in iNaturalist. Interviews with 31 young people examined how participation in the field and online supported learning.
  5. Tzathas et al. (2026), Adaptive Spatio-Temporal 3D Gaussian Splatting for Scenes with Oscillatory Motion. Research on reconstructing changing scenes, including leaves moving in the wind.
