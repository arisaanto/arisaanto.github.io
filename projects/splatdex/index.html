Splatdex

A Pokédex for splats

Personal project, 2026

The first Gaussian splats I encountered were Dany Bittel’s macro captures. I was drawn to the stillness of the subject and the movement available to the viewer. A fly could be approached from several directions, its hairs and wings available to an attention that would rarely be possible in passing. There was something of the natural history museum in it, the chance to linger with a form usually glimpsed in motion.1

Collections give scattered encounters a place in a larger world. Albums, field guides, and cabinets of curiosities each offer a way of keeping things together, returning to them, and noticing relations between them. For me, that pleasure first took shape in sticker books and the Pokédex. Pokémon Go later brought the impulse outdoors. I imagined a collection in which a shell, a flower, or a stone could become a spatial image to keep and share.

Where a photograph fixes a view, a splat allows movement through the views that were captured. A surface passes out of sight, another comes forward, and a detail begins to suggest a question. I wanted to give this kind of image a place to be kept, arranged, and read alongside other images. Splatdex brings captures from SuperSplat into catalogs, personal collections, and annotated views.

Notes bring a feature into view and connect it to an explanation. They stay attached to the object, so a reader can move between the whole and a particular detail.

Each note can also open a conversation with an optional AI assistant. The selected object and annotation give the question a context, whether it concerns a part, an unfamiliar term, or a connection beyond the initial explanation.

Research on interactive 3D displays suggests that both the views available and the way a learner encounters them matter. Active rotation helped object recognition in one VR study, while access to informative views explained benefits in another spatial task. These findings informed the combination of free exploration and annotated viewpoints.2 3

For my own collection, I chose objects that had stayed with me from different journeys. A reddish stone came from the Rota Vicentina in Portugal, walked with friends during my study abroad. The green-blue stone came from a stream near Bergün, toward the end of the Kesch Trek with a friend. The shell came from Wineglass Bay in Tasmania, where I spent last Christmas with my family. Their places in the collection carry something of those journeys.

A collection could hold personal histories as easily as information about an object. A shell might belong to a journal, a shared catalog, or a study guide. An annotation could record where it was found, explain its structure, or preserve a question to return to. Field observation and online exchange already meet in communities such as iNaturalist. A collection of spatial images could give that exchange another form.4

Splatdex brings existing capture and rendering tools into an interface for collecting, arranging, and annotating spatial images. I imagine contributors extending the explanations around an object, with AI helping readers pursue their questions. In AR, these collections could occupy the space around us. Research into dynamic splats also points toward studying movement alongside form.5

The current prototype includes editable annotations and an optional language model running in the browser. Automatic recognition, generated notes, shared study spaces, and AR remain directions for the project.

Notes

  1. Dany Bittel, Macro Splats. The macro captures that introduced me to the medium. The original Gaussian Splatting paper describes the underlying representation and rendering method.
  2. James et al. (2002), “Active” and “passive” learning of three-dimensional object structure within an immersive virtual reality environment. Participants recognized objects faster after learning their structure through active rotation.
  3. Keehner et al. (2008), Spatial Reasoning With External Visualizations. In their cross-section task, access to relevant views accounted for benefits that control alone did not explain.
  4. Herodotou et al., Young people in iNaturalist. Interviews with 31 young people examined how participation in the field and online supported learning.
  5. Tzathas et al. (2026), Adaptive Spatio-Temporal 3D Gaussian Splatting for Scenes with Oscillatory Motion. Research on reconstructing changing scenes, including leaves moving in the wind.
