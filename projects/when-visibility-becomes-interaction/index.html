Spatial user research · Emergency department

When Visibility Becomes Interaction

Can a proposed change to a building be tested through the people who will use it, before the walls are changed?

Aris Antoniadis · supervised by Leonel Aguilar Melgar (ETH Zurich) and Christoph Hölscher (ETH Zurich)

September 2026 · Research Project 1, University of Amsterdam · Conducted with the ETH Zurich Chair of Cognitive Science

Read the full report (PDF) Explore the code and results Explore the simulated personas

The question

An emergency department depends on people being able to find and speak to one another while clinical work continues around them. A transparent wall may make colleagues easier to see, but that does not tell us whether anyone will approach, who will have to walk farther, or whether the extra contact helps care. I wanted to test a proposed spatial change across that chain of events, while keeping its unsupported consequences visible.

What I built

I developed a spatial agent-based model of a University Hospital Zurich emergency-department care area. Staff follow role-specific tasks and routes through a floor plan. Visibility, a work-related reason, availability, and an approach within 1.5 m are all required before a face-to-face interaction is logged. The model was informed by the department’s layout, operational information, and 359 located interactions from staff shadowing.

The study had three connected parts. First, I compared the baseline simulation with observed interaction partners, topics, and locations. Next, I tested transparent partitions around the central coordination station, relocation of a smaller nurse station, and both changes together under matched patient-arrival streams. Finally, five deliberately different simulated personas made a fixed sample of optional contact decisions and appraised their completed shifts. The language model could choose among feasible actions; it did not control movement, clinical tasks, or patient outcomes.

What changed in the model

+9.07/hface-to-face contacts after central-station transparency under normal load
+7.07/hface-to-face contacts under high load
60–73%of additional walking in the combined condition fell to the coordination nurse

The extra contact was overwhelmingly between staff. Patient-facing contact stayed nearly constant in absolute terms. Relocating the smaller station produced little change in interaction: the new position rarely intersected with the work that would give staff a reason to use it. Transparency, by contrast, opened sightlines where work already brought relevant colleagues together. The coordination gain came with more total walking and an uneven burden on one role.

The same design, different simulated users

Part 3 explored a further question: would people with different priorities use the same spatial opportunity in the same way? The five personas were designed for this experiment, not derived from Zurich staff. Their clinical roles, staff positions, workload, and patient arrivals were matched across layout conditions. They experienced similar gains in time with a visible colleague, yet their additional initiated contacts differed. In a separate check, all five personas received the same 120 decision situations; they chose different actions in 96 of them.

Where the model stops

The baseline reproduced broad interaction hotspots better than exact locations and outperformed random spatial controls. Its simulated focal interaction rate, however, was well below the rate estimated from shadowing records. The experimental effect sizes should be read as results inside a controlled model, not predictions for the real department.

More importantly, the model has no evidence-backed mechanism by which an extra exchange changes a handover, clinical decision, task duration, or patient outcome. The project can trace a layout change through visibility, movement, and coordination; it cannot establish that patients benefit. Nor do the five designed personas stand in for measured differences among real staff.

Why this matters to my work

This project was my first attempt to treat a physical environment as an interface that can be tested with different users before changing it. A more useful system would pair simulations with direct observation of how people actually navigate, coordinate, and recover from interruption, then return its predictions to those people for challenge and revision. That same research problem extends beyond hospitals to homes, vehicles, public spaces, and the technologies embedded in them: a spatial interface matters through the activities it enables and the burdens it redistributes.
